Chapter 0 · The Primer

How Economists Watch Movies

This chapter
The method & the paper
Before you screen anything
Read this first
A physicist, a psychologist, and an economist walk out of the same two-hour film. The physicist noticed the space station spun at the wrong speed. The psychologist watched a marriage quietly fall apart. The economist? She spent the whole film asking one question — who wanted what, and what were they willing to give up to get it?

That question is the whole enterprise in miniature. Economics is not, at its heart, about money. It is about choice under scarcity: how people decide when they cannot have everything, how those decisions collide, and what happens to all of us as a result. Money is just the most convenient place to watch the drama unfold. A film, it turns out, is another.

Over the next thirteen chapters you will watch thirteen movies — a Pixar romance set on a garbage planet, a Cold War satire, a 1930s dust-bowl migration, a Wall Street morality play — and behind each one you will find a piece of the professional economics literature: a Nobel lecture, a paper that reshaped a field, an argument economists still have at conferences. The films are the hook. The papers are the substance. This book lives in the space between them.

Why film

The movie is a laboratory you can't build

Economists face a frustrating problem: they cannot run experiments the way a chemist can. You cannot put a whole economy in a test tube, take the gold standard away from one copy and leave it in another, and watch what happens. So economists reach for the next best things — history, data, mathematical models, and, when they are lucky, a vivid story that makes an abstract force visible.

A good film does something a supply-and-demand diagram cannot: it makes you feel the incentive. When you watch a bank run in It's a Wonderful Life, you understand in your gut why a depositor sprints to the teller window even though the sprint is exactly what dooms the bank. That gut understanding is the beginning of economic reasoning. The paper then gives you the machinery to explain it precisely.

The core move of this book

For every film, ask: What is the economic problem here? Then reach for the assigned reading, which names that problem, models it, and tells you what economists have learned about it. The film supplies the intuition; the paper supplies the rigor.

The toolkit

Five ideas you'll use again and again

You do not need advanced mathematics for this book — you need a handful of concepts and the discipline to apply them honestly. Five will carry you a long way:

The recurring toolkit
Incentives
People respond to costs and benefits at the margin. Change the payoff and you change the behavior — usually in ways the well-meaning planner didn't intend.
Opportunity cost
The true cost of anything is what you gave up to get it. Every "yes" is a "no" to something else.
Externalities
When my choice spills a cost or benefit onto you that no price accounts for — pollution, vaccination, a neighbor's garden — markets misfire.
Information
Who knows what matters enormously. When one side of a deal knows more than the other, ordinary markets can break.
Strategic interaction
When your best move depends on my move and mine on yours, we're in a game — and the outcome can be bad for everyone even when everyone plays smart.

Every film in this book is a stress test of one or more of these ideas. WALL·E is externalities and the commons. Dr. Strangelove is strategic interaction. The Big Short is information and incentives gone rotten. Keep the toolkit in your pocket; you will reach for it constantly.

Watch like an economist

Read the article before the screening

This is the single most important piece of advice in the chapter, so it gets its own paragraph. Read the assigned article before you watch the film. The reason is simple: once you know what an economist is looking for, you see it everywhere on screen. Watch Intolerable Cruelty after reading Akerlof and every scene becomes a study in who is hiding what. Watch it cold and it is merely a screwball comedy about divorce lawyers.

As you watch, keep a running list of moments where the economics is right, where it is dramatized (bent for the sake of story), and where it is simply wrong. That list is the raw material for your paper. Directors are not economists; part of your job is to referee the difference, generously but honestly.

✎ The paper: the heart of the work

Most instructors who teach with this book assign short analytical papers — typically 500 to 1,000 words each. A paper discusses the economic content of a film in light of the assigned reading (and any class discussion). It is not a movie review. "The acting was wooden" earns nothing; "the film gets the mechanics of a bank run right but wrongly implies deposit insurance would have saved the Bailey Building & Loan in 1932" is the whole game.

Craft

What a strong paper actually does

The best papers share a shape. They open by naming the economic problem the film dramatizes. They explain the relevant model or argument from the reading in the student's own words — briefly, correctly, with the key term used precisely. They then point to specific scenes as evidence, and they take a position: does the film illuminate the economics, distort it, or quietly assume something the economist would challenge? They close with a claim worth making.

Weak papers, by contrast, retell the plot, praise the cinematography, or summarize the article without ever connecting it to a frame of film. Avoid all three. Your reader has seen the movie and read the paper; your job is to say something about the relationship between them that they hadn't quite noticed.

Paper mechanics (typical)
Length
500–1,000 words. Tight and argued beats long and loose.
Format
Double-spaced, 12-point, your choice of font. Submit however your instructor directs.
Citations
Chicago style. Cite the article whenever you lean on it. Spelling and grammar count.
Deadlines
Follow your instructor's deadlines — many treat late work strictly, so plan your calendar.
Integrity
Your analysis must be yours. Cite your sources, and follow your institution's academic-honesty and AI-use policies.
The film gives you the intuition. The paper gives you the proof. Your essay lives in between.
The method of this book

One last thing before the lights go down. Economics has a reputation for cynicism — the "dismal science," always reducing human beings to calculating machines. You will feel that tension in these chapters, never more than when we reach crime, capital punishment, and the social responsibility of business. Hold onto the discomfort. The most interesting papers you will write are the ones where the economic logic and your moral intuition pull in different directions, and you have to decide, carefully, what to make of the gap.

Now — Chapter 1. A little robot is alone on an abandoned Earth, compacting the trash we left behind. The question that opens the whole book is waiting in the rubble: how did it come to this, and could anything have stopped it?

Chapter 1 · Resource Economics

The Tragedy of the Commons

Film
WALL·E · 2008 · 98 min
Reading
Hardin (1968) · Ostrom et al. (1999)
The camera drifts over a dead city. Skyscrapers turn out to be towers of compacted garbage, stacked by a single rusting robot who has been tidying an abandoned planet for 700 years. Nobody meant to bury the Earth. Everybody, doing exactly what made sense for themselves, did it together.

WALL·E never mentions economics, but its opening twenty minutes are the most beautiful illustration of a single idea that anyone has ever animated: the tragedy of the commons. A resource that belongs to everyone — the atmosphere, the oceans, an empty planet's capacity to absorb our waste — tends to be used until it is destroyed, not because people are wicked, but because each person's share of the damage is tiny while their share of the benefit is whole.

The humans of WALL·E are not villains. They are comfortable, distracted passengers on the starship Axiom, each having made a perfectly reasonable series of small choices — buy the convenient thing, discard the used thing, let the megacorporation handle it — that added up to a ruined home. That gap between sensible individual choices and a catastrophic collective outcome is where this chapter lives.

The economic idea

Why shared resources get destroyed

Imagine a pasture open to all. Each herder gains the full value of every animal they add to the herd, but the cost of overgrazing — the thinning grass — is spread across every herder who uses the field. So each herder, reasoning honestly, concludes: add another animal. The benefit is mine; the cost is ours. Every herder reasons the same way. The grass dies. This is Garrett Hardin's parable, and its engine is not greed but arithmetic: private benefit, socialized cost.

The fishery below is the same story in water. Drag the slider and watch what happens as more boats join a shared sea. Up to a point, more effort means more fish for everyone. Past that point, the boats are competing for a shrinking stock — and because no one owns the sea, no one has any reason to stop.

The reading, unpacked

Hardin's warning — and its blind spot

Garrett Hardin published "The Tragedy of the Commons" in Science in 1968, and it became one of the most cited papers in the history of the journal. His argument was stark. In a world of finite resources and growing population, any commons — grazing land, fisheries, the air, the planet itself — is doomed to ruin if left open to unrestricted use. "Freedom in a commons," he wrote, "brings ruin to all." His proposed remedies were equally stark: either convert the commons into private property (give someone a reason to conserve it) or subject it to government control ("mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon").

Hardin's diagnosis was powerful and largely right. His menu of cures, it turned out, was too short — and that is where the second reading comes in.

ⓘ The Money Behind the Scene

The Axiom is run by Buy n Large, a single megacorporation that has absorbed governments and privatized nearly everything. Notice the irony: WALL·E's Earth is not a lawless free-for-all — it is the opposite, a world where one owner controlled the commons and still destroyed it, because its incentive was quarterly convenience, not centuries-long stewardship. Privatization is not a magic fix if the private owner's horizon is short.

The counterpoint

Ostrom: the commons that governed themselves

In 1999, Elinor Ostrom and her colleagues published "Revisiting the Commons" in Science — a direct, evidence-based reply to Hardin. Ostrom had spent her career doing something Hardin never did: looking at actual commons. Swiss alpine pastures managed continuously for 500 years. Japanese village forests. Spanish irrigation districts. Maine lobster fisheries. In case after case, she found communities that had not destroyed their shared resources, and had not needed either full privatization or a distant government to do it.

They had governed themselves. Ostrom identified the design principles that made it work: clearly defined boundaries about who may use the resource; rules matched to local conditions; the users themselves participating in making those rules; monitoring; graduated sanctions for those who break the rules; cheap ways to resolve conflicts. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics, largely for this work. Her message: Hardin's tragedy is a real danger, but it is a possibility, not a law of nature. Human beings can and do build institutions to escape it.

Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all.
Garrett Hardin, 1968 — the sentence Ostrom spent her life complicating
The film as evidence

What WALL·E gets right — and what it skips

WALL·E is Hardin animated. The dead Earth is the ruined commons; the humans' cheerful obliviousness is the "private benefit, socialized cost" logic playing out over generations. The film even nods at Hardin's warning about how comfort and technology can numb us to the accumulating damage.

But watch for what the film leaves out — and this is exactly the kind of observation a strong paper is built on. There is no Ostrom in WALL·E. There are no communities negotiating rules, no monitoring, no graduated sanctions, no messy democratic self-governance. The film offers only two futures: passive ruin, or a single redemptive act (a plant, a captain's change of heart). Ostrom's whole point is that the interesting solutions live in the vast middle ground the film skips — the boring, durable institutions that ordinary people build to manage what they share. Is the film's pessimism about collective action earned, or is it a failure of imagination? That question is a paper.

Key terms
Commons
A resource open to use by many, from which it is hard to exclude anyone (a fishery, the atmosphere).
Rivalry
One person's use subtracts from what's left for others — the feature that makes a commons vulnerable.
Free-rider
Someone who takes the benefit of a shared resource while dodging the cost of maintaining it.
Externality
A cost (or benefit) of a choice that falls on people who had no say in it.
Design principles
Ostrom's checklist — boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions — for commons that govern themselves.
Prompts for your paper
Pick one, or let it spark your own. 500–1,000 words, Chicago citations, on the economics — not a review.
  1. WALL·E dramatizes Hardin but never Ostrom. Using specific scenes, argue whether the film's fatalism about the commons is justified or whether it ignores the self-governance Ostrom documented.
  2. Buy n Large privatized nearly everything and still ruined the Earth. Does the film support or undercut Hardin's claim that private property protects a commons? Bring in the idea of the owner's time horizon.
  3. Identify the "commons" in the film precisely — is it the Earth, the atmosphere, or the Axiom's own resources? How does your choice change which of Hardin's or Ostrom's remedies would apply?
Chapter 2 · Environmental Economics

The Problem of Social Cost

Film
A Civil Action · 1998 · 115 min
Reading
Coase (1960)
Two children are dead in Woburn, Massachusetts, and a hotshot personal-injury lawyer thinks he smells a payday. What he finds instead is trichloroethylene in the town wells, two corporate giants upstream, and a legal system trying to answer a question economists had been circling for decades: when one party's activity harms another, who should pay — and does it even matter who we blame?

A Civil Action is a courtroom drama about pollution, but underneath the depositions it is dramatizing the central puzzle of environmental economics. A tannery and a food-products plant put solvents into the ground. The solvents reach the water. Children get leukemia. The activity that created value for the companies imposed a devastating cost on the families — a cost that appeared nowhere in the price of leather or food. Economists call that uncounted cost an externality, and the question of what to do about it produced one of the most influential — and most misunderstood — papers in the history of the field.

The economic idea

Externalities and the reciprocal nature of harm

The old, intuitive view — associated with the economist A.C. Pigou — was simple: a factory that pollutes imposes a cost on others, so the government should tax the factory by the amount of the harm. Make the polluter pay, and the market price will finally tell the truth. Pigou's logic still underlies carbon taxes today, and it is not wrong.

Ronald Coase, writing in 1960, made a disorienting observation that reframed the whole problem. Harm, he insisted, is reciprocal. We say the factory harms the residents — but to stop the factory is to harm the factory (and its workers, and its customers). The rancher's cattle trample the farmer's crops; but fencing the cattle harms the rancher. The real economic question is not "who is the villain?" It is "which use of the resource is more valuable, and how do we get there at the lowest cost?"

The Coase Theorem

If property rights are clearly defined and people can bargain at low cost, they will negotiate their way to the efficient outcome — the one that maximizes total value — regardless of who was initially assigned the right. The initial allocation determines who pays whom; it does not determine what ultimately happens.

See it work

Bargaining to efficiency — when it's cheap

Suppose a factory's production is worth $100 to the factory but its pollution costs a downstream laundry $60. The efficient outcome is that the factory keeps operating (society nets $40). Coase's claim: the parties reach that outcome by bargaining whether the law gives the factory a right to pollute or gives the laundry a right to clean water. What changes is only who writes whom a check. The catch is buried in a phrase easy to miss: "at low cost."

The reading, unpacked

What Coase actually argued

Here is the irony that trips up most readers. "The Problem of Social Cost" is usually remembered for the frictionless-bargaining result — the "Coase Theorem" — as if Coase believed the world worked that way. He did not. Coase used the zero-transaction-cost world as a foil. His real message was the opposite: in the actual world, transaction costs are everywhere and often enormous — the cost of finding all the affected parties, negotiating with them, writing and enforcing agreements. Because bargaining is costly, the initial assignment of rights, and the design of legal rules, genuinely matters. The law should try to place rights where they will do the most good, because parties often cannot cheaply rearrange them.

Coase's deeper contribution was to make economists take institutions — laws, courts, firms, contracts — seriously as the machinery that determines whether resources reach their highest-value use. He won the 1991 Nobel Prize for this and related work. Read him carefully and you find not a libertarian fantasy of costless markets but a sober argument that the friction is the point.

✎ What the film gets right about transaction costs

A Civil Action is, in effect, a two-hour illustration of transaction costs devouring everyone. The families cannot simply "bargain" with W.R. Grace and Beatrice Foods — the harm is diffuse, causation is scientifically murky, the parties are wildly unequal in resources, and the only forum is ruinously expensive litigation. Jan Schlichtmann bankrupts his firm just to establish who caused what. Coase would recognize this instantly: when transaction costs are this high, the tidy theorem is useless and the legal rules — burden of proof, liability standards — decide everything.

The film as evidence

Litigation as the most expensive bargain of all

Watch the film with Coase in mind and a strong essay almost writes itself. The lawsuit is the bargaining process — a monstrously costly one. Every obstacle Schlichtmann hits (the cost of expert hydrogeologists, the asymmetry between his small firm and corporate defense teams, the difficulty of proving the solvents caused these specific cancers) is a transaction cost standing between the town and the efficient, just outcome. The film's tragedy is not that the villains escaped a Pigouvian tax; it is that the frictions were so large the right outcome was never reachable through the mechanism available.

A subtler paper might ask: would a Pigovian pollution tax, set in advance, have served Woburn better than the tort system's after-the-fact bargaining? Or would deterrence through liability have worked if proof were cheaper? These are live debates in environmental economics, and the film hands you the case study.

Key terms
Externality
A cost or benefit imposed on a third party not reflected in prices.
Transaction costs
The costs of reaching, writing, and enforcing an agreement — search, negotiation, litigation.
Coase Theorem
With clear rights and costless bargaining, efficiency results regardless of the initial allocation.
Property rights
The legally recognized entitlement to use, exclude from, or transfer a resource.
Pigovian tax
A tax equal to the external harm, meant to make the price tell the truth.
Prompts for your paper
Pick one. Discuss the economics of the film in light of Coase — not the courtroom tactics.
  1. A Civil Action is often read as a story about corporate villainy. Reframe it, using Coase, as a story about transaction costs. Which reading better explains the outcome?
  2. Coase argued the initial assignment of rights matters most when bargaining is costly. Trace how the legal rules in the film (burden of proof, causation standards) allocated de facto rights — and shaped the result.
  3. Would a Pigovian tax on solvent use have protected Woburn better than the tort system? Argue the trade-off between taxing pollution up front and litigating harm after the fact.
Chapter 3 · Game Theory

War, Peace, and the Logic of Deterrence

Film
Dr. Strangelove · 1964 · 93 min
Reading
Aumann, Nobel Lecture (2005)
"Gentlemen, you can't fight in here — this is the War Room." A single deranged general has launched the bombers. The Soviets, it emerges, have built a Doomsday Machine that will automatically end all life if attacked — but forgot to tell anyone. A deterrent nobody knows about deters nothing. The world ends on a technicality of game theory.

Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove is the funniest film ever made about the end of the world, and it is also a remarkably precise seminar in strategic thinking. Every catastrophe in the film flows from a failure of the logic of deterrence — the branch of game theory that asks how rational adversaries, each able to destroy the other, might nonetheless keep the peace. Robert Aumann spent a career on exactly that question and won the 2005 Nobel Prize for it. His lecture, "War and Peace," is your reading, and its conclusions are more hopeful — and stranger — than the film's mushroom cloud suggests.

The economic idea

When my best move depends on yours

Game theory is the study of strategic interaction: situations where your best choice depends on what I do, and mine on what you do. Its most famous scenario is the Prisoner's Dilemma, and the Cold War arms race is its most consequential real-world instance. Two superpowers each choose to arm or disarm. Whatever the other does, each is individually better off arming — and so both arm, both spend fortunes, both end up less safe than if they had both disarmed. Rational individual choices, collectively disastrous.

The reading, unpacked

Aumann: repetition changes everything

If the game is played once, the Prisoner's Dilemma is grim: defection is inescapable. Robert Aumann's great contribution was to study what happens when the same players face each other again and again, with no known final round. His "Folk Theorem" results show that repetition can rescue cooperation. If both sides value the future enough, mutual restraint becomes self-enforcing: I refrain from attacking you not out of goodwill but because I know that an attack today buys me a lifetime of retaliation tomorrow. Peace, in this view, is not the absence of conflict but a stable equilibrium sustained by the threat of future punishment.

The slider below makes the knife-edge visible. When players weigh the future heavily (high patience), cooperation pays more than a one-time betrayal, and peace holds. When they discount the future steeply — because they are reckless, or because a first strike might be decisive — the temptation to defect wins, and deterrence collapses.

The Nobel argument

"War and Peace" and the paradox of deterrence

Aumann's lecture delivers a conclusion many find uncomfortable. Repeated-game theory implies that lasting peace may require credible commitments to retaliate — sometimes even the willingness to build weapons you devoutly hope never to use. A disarmament that removes the capacity to punish defection can, paradoxically, make war more likely by removing the threat that held it in check. Aumann pointedly argued that a naïve, one-sided rush to peace can be destabilizing, and that the durable Cold War peace rested on mutual, credible deterrence — mutually assured destruction, coldly rational.

Repeated games… the great engine that turns the prisoner's dilemma of the moment into the possibility of peace.
after Robert Aumann, Nobel Lecture, 2005
ⓘ Credibility is everything

The Doomsday Machine is a masterclass in one word: credibility. A deterrent works only if the adversary knows it exists and believes it will fire. The Soviets built an automatic, un-recallable retaliation — brilliant, in theory, because it removes the human hesitation that might make a threat hollow. Then they kept it secret. Dr. Strangelove himself delivers the verdict: "The whole point of a Doomsday Machine is lost if you keep it a secret! Why didn't you tell the world?!" A credible threat unannounced is the strategic equivalent of a lock with no keyhole.

The film as evidence

Every disaster is a game-theory failure

Dr. Strangelove is a catalogue of the ways deterrence breaks. The un-recallable order (a commitment device that removes flexibility and becomes a liability). The secret deterrent (a threat that can't deter because it isn't known). The single rogue actor able to trigger catastrophe (a failure of what economists call command and control over the retaliation mechanism). The film argues that the rational architecture of MAD is only as safe as its weakest human and mechanical link — a critique that runs against Aumann's confidence in equilibrium.

The richest papers on this chapter live in that tension. Aumann says credible mutual deterrence keeps the peace; Kubrick says the machinery of credible deterrence is so brittle that it will eventually, absurdly, kill us all. Both are arguments about the same equilibrium. Which one does the twentieth century's actual record support? You now have the tools to take a side.

Key terms
Nash equilibrium
A set of strategies where no player can do better by changing theirs alone.
Prisoner's Dilemma
A game where individually rational defection yields a collectively worse outcome.
Repeated game
The same game played many times, enabling cooperation through future rewards and punishments.
Credible commitment
A threat or promise an adversary believes will actually be carried out.
Deterrence (MAD)
Preventing attack by guaranteeing unacceptable retaliation.
Prompts for your paper
Discuss the strategic logic — not the satire's jokes, except as evidence.
  1. Aumann argues credible deterrence sustains peace; the film argues the machinery of deterrence is fatally brittle. Using specific scenes, judge whose model the Cold War record vindicates.
  2. Analyze the Doomsday Machine as a commitment device. Why does removing your own ability to back down sometimes strengthen a deterrent — and when does it backfire?
  3. Map the arms race onto the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. What real feature of nuclear conflict most threatens the "value the future enough" condition that makes cooperation possible?
Chapter 4 · Asymmetric Information

The Market for Lemons

Film
Intolerable Cruelty · 2003 · 100 min
Reading
Akerlof (1970)
Miles Massey is the most feared divorce attorney in Los Angeles, author of an ironclad prenuptial agreement no one has ever broken. Marylin Rexroth is a professional divorcée who marries wealth for a living. Each is certain they know the other's game. Neither knows the other's real information. The entire film is a courtship conducted in the dark — a screwball comedy about the economics of not knowing what your partner is worth.

The Coen Brothers made Intolerable Cruelty as a battle of wits between two people who lie for a living, and in doing so they built a near-perfect illustration of the problem that won George Akerlof a Nobel Prize: what happens to a market when one side knows something the other doesn't. In the film it is marriage, prenups, and hidden motives. In Akerlof's 1970 paper it is used cars. The mechanism is identical, and once you see it you will find it everywhere — in insurance, in hiring, in finance, in love.

The economic idea

When one side knows more than the other

Standard economics often assumes buyers and sellers are equally informed. Drop that assumption and markets can misbehave badly. Asymmetric information is the condition where one party to a transaction knows something relevant that the other cannot verify — the seller knows the car is a wreck; the applicant knows they are a reckless driver; the spouse knows they married for money. The danger this creates is called adverse selection: the very people you least want to trade with are the ones most eager to trade.

The reading, unpacked

Akerlof and the unraveling market

George Akerlof's "The Market for Lemons" (1970) was rejected by three journals before it was published — editors thought it was either trivial or wrong. It is now one of the most cited papers in economics. Its argument is a small, devastating piece of logic. Suppose used cars come in two kinds: good ones ("peaches") and bad ones ("lemons"). Sellers know which they have; buyers cannot tell them apart. A buyer, unable to distinguish, will only pay the price for an average car. But at an average price, the owners of peaches — whose cars are worth more than average — withdraw from the market. Now only lemons are left. The average quality drops, so the price a buyer will pay drops, so the next-best cars withdraw… The market can unravel until only the worst goods trade, or until it disappears entirely.

Drag the slider to watch it happen. As the share of good cars falls, the price a rational buyer offers falls below what a peach owner will accept — and the good cars vanish.

The cures

Signals, screening, and warranties

Akerlof's paper launched a whole field devoted to how markets fight back against the lemons problem. The informed party can signal quality with a costly, credible action — a used-car dealer offers a warranty (cheap to give on a peach, ruinous on a lemon, so only peach-sellers offer it). The uninformed party can screen, designing choices that make the other side reveal what they know (an insurer offers a menu of deductibles; safe drivers pick the high deductible). Reputation, certification, brands, licenses, inspections — a huge share of the modern economy exists precisely to fix the problem Akerlof identified. Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel with Akerlof for working out signaling and screening; Chapter 7 of your course, on signaling in StrategyArena's sibling curriculum, extends this directly.

✎ The prenup as a screening device

Watch Massey's famous "Massey Prenup" through Akerlof's eyes. A prenuptial agreement is a screening mechanism: it changes the payoff to marrying for money, so it separates partners who want the person from partners who want the estate. When Massey tears up the prenup as a declaration of love, he is doing something an economist finds thrilling — deliberately destroying his own information protection to send an unfakeable signal of sincerity. It's costly (it exposes him to exactly the predation he built his career defending against), which is precisely what makes it credible.

The film as evidence

Two experts trapped in each other's information

Intolerable Cruelty is asymmetric information dramatized as romance. Every major turn is a move in an information game: a hidden motive revealed, a fake identity, a bluff about wealth, a signal that may or may not be sincere. Both leads are professionals at exploiting what others don't know — which makes them uniquely unable to trust each other, because each knows exactly how the con is run. The comedy comes from two people who cannot take any signal at face value, because they both manufacture false signals for a living. That is adverse selection in the marriage market: the people most skilled at the game are the ones you'd least want to play it with.

A sharp paper will pick a single scene — the prenup destruction, a staged seduction, a bluff about assets — and analyze it precisely as signaling or screening, asking whether the "signal" is credible in Akerlof's technical sense (is it genuinely too costly to fake?). The film is generous with material; your job is to bring the model to the frame.

Key terms
Asymmetric information
One party knows something relevant the other cannot verify.
Adverse selection
Hidden quality drives good types out of the market, leaving the worst.
Signaling
The informed side takes a costly action credible only for good types (a warranty, a degree).
Screening
The uninformed side offers choices that make the other reveal their type.
Moral hazard
Hidden action (vs. hidden type): once insured, people take more risk.
Prompts for your paper
Analyze a scene as an information problem — not the romance for its own sake.
  1. Read the "Massey Prenup" and its destruction as, respectively, a screening device and a signal. Is the signal credible in Akerlof's sense? What makes it costly enough to be believed?
  2. Does the marriage market in the film exhibit adverse selection? Identify who the "peaches" and "lemons" are and whether the good types are driven out.
  3. Compare the lemons problem in used cars to the trust problem between the two leads. What "warranties" or reputational signals do the characters use to trade despite mutual suspicion?
Chapter 5 · Monetary Systems

How Money Is Born

Film
Stalag 17 · 1953 · 120 min
Reading
Radford (1945)
In a German POW camp, a barracks of American airmen runs a black market, a betting ring, and a distillery. Sergeant Sefton, the barracks cynic, trades cigarettes for eggs, eggs for silk, and information for whatever it's worth — and everyone hates him for being good at it. Nobody printed a single dollar. Yet the camp has a currency, prices, inflation, and a class structure. Where did the money come from?

Stalag 17 is a prisoner-of-war thriller, but it is also, almost accidentally, a documentary about the spontaneous birth of money. The men have no government, no central bank, no legal tender — and within weeks they have all the machinery of a monetary economy. That is not a Hollywood invention. It is precisely what a young British economist named R.A. Radford lived through and then described, in 1945, in one of the most beloved short papers in the discipline: "The Economic Organisation of a P.O.W. Camp."

The economic idea

What money actually is

Money is not the paper. Money is anything that reliably performs three jobs: a medium of exchange (everyone will accept it, so you don't need to barter), a unit of account (you can quote prices in it), and a store of value (it holds worth between the time you earn it and the time you spend it). Anything that does those three jobs is money, whether it's gold, dollars, or — in Radford's camp — cigarettes.

Why cigarettes? They were durable, roughly uniform in quality, easily divisible (down to a single cigarette, or even fractions), portable, and in limited supply. They had intrinsic value to smokers, which bootstrapped their acceptance, but they quickly became valuable to non-smokers too, purely because everyone else would take them. That last step — value arising from universal acceptance rather than intrinsic use — is the moment a commodity becomes money.

The reading, unpacked

Radford's camp: a whole economy in miniature

Radford observed that the camp economy developed every feature of the outside world. Prices for coffee, chocolate, and jam settled into stable exchange rates quoted in cigarettes, posted on a bulletin board and arbitraged across barracks. A middleman class emerged (Sefton, exactly). Credit appeared. There was even a shop that issued paper receipts backed by a cigarette reserve — a primitive central bank. And, crucially, the camp suffered inflation and deflation driven entirely by the money supply.

When a Red Cross parcel delivery arrived, the camp was suddenly flooded with cigarettes: prices of everything (in cigarettes) jumped — inflation, with no more actual goods to buy. In the lean days before the next delivery, cigarettes grew scarce, prices fell, and trade slowed — deflation. Move the slider and watch the price level track the money supply, exactly as the quantity theory of money predicts.

ⓘ Gresham's Law in the barracks

Watch what happens to quality. When cigarettes are money, people pay with the worst cigarettes and smoke or hoard the best — "bad money drives out good," Gresham's Law. Machine-rolled brands circulated as currency while hand-rolled or premium smokes disappeared into private stashes. Radford noticed this; so can you, in any scene where a prisoner sorts his cigarettes into "to spend" and "to keep."

The film as evidence

Sefton, the man who understood money

Sefton is the film's most interesting economic character precisely because the other prisoners despise him for behavior that is, economically, rational and even socially useful. He arbitrages (buys where cheap, sells where dear), he provides liquidity (he'll always make a market), he extends credit, and he prices risk (his betting operation). The barracks reads this as collaboration and greed; an economist reads it as the ordinary, unglamorous work of a functioning market. The film invites you to judge him morally — and a strong paper will ask whether the moral judgment and the economic function can be separated.

The deeper point for your essay: the camp had no state, no coercion behind its money, no legal tender laws — and money emerged anyway, because the men needed to trade and barter is unbearably inefficient. That is a profound argument about the origins of money, and it cuts against the idea that money requires a government to create it. Then again, the camp's "central bank" collapsed in a bank run when confidence failed. What does that tell you about how much money depends on trust?

Medium of exchange
Something widely accepted in trade, eliminating the need for barter.
Commodity money
Money that is also a useful good (cigarettes, gold, salt).
Quantity theory of money
Other things equal, the price level moves with the money supply.
Gresham's Law
When two monies circulate, people spend the inferior and hoard the superior.
Liquidity
How easily an asset can be exchanged for goods without losing value.

Chapter 6 · The Gold Standard

The Wonderful Wizard of the Gold Standard

Film
The Wizard of Oz · 1939 · 101 min
Reading
Rockoff (1990)
A girl from Kansas follows a road of yellow brick, in silver shoes (they were silver in the book, before Technicolor turned them ruby), toward an Emerald City where a Wizard rules by illusion. She is joined by a brainless farmer, a heartless industrial worker, and a cowardly politician. It is a children's fairy tale. It may also be the most elaborate monetary allegory in American literature.

In 1990, the economic historian Hugh Rockoff published "The Wizard of Oz as a Monetary Allegory" in the Journal of Political Economy, reviving a reading first suggested decades earlier: that L. Frank Baum's 1900 novel is a coded parable about the fiercest economic debate of its era — whether the United States should stay on the gold standard or expand the money supply by coining silver. Whether or not Baum intended every symbol, the film gives us an unbeatable vehicle for understanding what a gold standard is and why it can quietly grind a farming nation into revolt.

The economic idea

Why the money standard matters

Under a gold standard, the dollar is defined as a fixed weight of gold, and the money supply can grow only as fast as the gold stock. That has a great virtue — it ties the government's hands, preventing it from printing money and creating inflation. But it has a cruel flaw. If the economy grows faster than the gold supply, there is too little money chasing too many goods, and prices must fall. Persistent deflation follows. And deflation is not neutral: it is a massive, silent transfer of wealth from debtors to creditors.

Here is why. A farmer borrows $1,000 to buy land, expecting to repay it by selling crops. If crop prices fall 30% through deflation, the $1,000 debt hasn't changed — but the farmer now needs far more bushels of wheat to earn each dollar. The real burden of the debt has ballooned, while the banker who lent the money is repaid in dollars worth more than the ones he lent. Drag the slider and feel the squeeze.

The reading, unpacked

Rockoff decodes the fairy tale

Rockoff maps the story onto the politics of the 1890s, when William Jennings Bryan thundered that mankind should not be "crucified upon a cross of gold" and demanded free coinage of silver to inflate the money supply and relieve debtors. In Rockoff's reading: Dorothy is the everyman American; Kansas is the drought- and debt-stricken heartland; the Scarecrow is the farmer (wrongly thought stupid, actually shrewd); the Tin Woodman is the industrial worker (dehumanized, "rusted" into unemployment); the Cowardly Lion is Bryan himself (a mighty roar, faint follow-through). The Yellow Brick Road is the gold standard; the silver shoes are the silver solution; the Emerald City is Washington, green as paper money; and the Wizard is the political establishment ruling by illusion. Dorothy's power was in the silver shoes all along — the money question, hiding in plain sight.

✎ Did Baum mean it?

Be honest in your paper about a real scholarly dispute: many literary historians doubt Baum intended a monetary allegory at all, and Rockoff himself is careful. This is a feature, not a bug, for an economics essay. The interesting question isn't "did Baum secretly encode silver policy?" but "does the allegory, intended or not, accurately capture the economics of the gold-standard debate?" You can grant that the symbolism may be coincidental and still use it to explain deflation, debt burdens, and the politics of the money supply with perfect rigor.

The film as evidence

Reading the road, the shoes, and the humbug

The film changes one crucial symbol: MGM made the silver slippers ruby because red popped against the yellow road in Technicolor. An allegory about silver, filmed without the silver — there is a paper in that irony alone. Beyond it, the film hands you the whole 1890s in images: a road of gold that leads only to a fraud, a heartland desperate for rain (and for easier credit), a wizard whose authority evaporates the moment the curtain is pulled. Whether or not Baum was a monetary cryptographer, the story dramatizes the core truth of the era — that the choice of monetary standard is never technical and never neutral. It decides who prospers and who is crushed.

Chapter 7 · Financial Panics

Why Banks Run

Film
It's a Wonderful Life · 1946 · 129 min
Reading
Silber (2009)
On what should be his wedding day, George Bailey stands between a panicked crowd and the door of the Bailey Building & Loan. "You're thinking of this place all wrong," he pleads. "The money's not here. Your money's in Joe's house, and in the Kennedy house, and a hundred others." He is explaining fractional-reserve banking to a mob that wants its cash now. It is the best lecture on financial panics ever put on film.

The run on the Bailey Building & Loan is a two-minute masterclass in why banks are simultaneously essential and fragile. George is right: a bank does not keep your deposits in a drawer. It lends them out. That is its entire social purpose — turning idle savings into houses and businesses. But it means no bank on earth can pay all its depositors at once. A bank is solvent and useful right up until the moment everyone believes it isn't — and then the belief makes itself true.

The economic idea

The self-fulfilling panic

Banks perform maturity transformation: they borrow short (deposits you can withdraw any time) and lend long (30-year mortgages). This creates enormous value but leaves the bank exposed to a coordination problem among depositors. If everyone stays calm, the bank is fine and everyone earns interest. If everyone panics and withdraws, the bank must dump its long-term loans at fire-sale prices, goes under, and the last people in line lose everything. The nightmare: even a perfectly healthy bank can be destroyed purely by the fear that others will run. There are two equilibria, and the bad one is a prophecy that fulfills itself.

The matrix below strips it to two depositors. Notice there is no single "right" answer — your best move depends entirely on what you expect the other to do.

The reading, unpacked

Silber: why the 1933 Bank Holiday worked

If panics are self-fulfilling, how do you stop one? William Silber's 2009 paper, "Why Did FDR's Bank Holiday Succeed?", answers with one of history's most dramatic examples. By March 1933, a nationwide run had shuttered the American banking system. Newly inaugurated, Franklin Roosevelt declared a bank holiday — he closed every bank for a week. Then, crucially, federal examiners inspected each bank and reopened only those judged sound, and Roosevelt used his first Fireside Chat to tell Americans, plainly, that the reopened banks were safe. Silber's insight: the holiday worked not merely as a pause but as a credible signal. The government put its own guarantee behind the surviving banks — and, backed by new emergency powers to supply currency, it changed what depositors expected everyone else to do. Deposits flowed back in. The bad equilibrium was broken by shifting beliefs.

ⓘ What actually saves a bank

The film shows George stopping the run with his own honeymoon cash and sheer force of personality — a beautiful story, and a fragile fix. Silber points to the durable solutions: a lender of last resort (a central bank that can supply cash in a pinch, which the Fed failed to do adequately before 1933) and deposit insurance (the FDIC, created that same year), which removes any reason to run because your deposit is guaranteed. After 1934, the classic contagious bank run largely vanished from America — not because banks got safer, but because the coordination problem was solved.

The film as evidence

George Bailey vs. the FDIC

Here is where a careful paper earns its keep. The film is set partly in the early 1930s, and George's run is stopped by personal heroism. But the real historical fix arrived precisely then — federal deposit insurance — and it would have protected his depositors far more reliably than his wedding money. The film celebrates the individual moral solution; the economics points to the institutional one. Is It's a Wonderful Life quietly nostalgic for a world of trustworthy local bankers that deposit insurance made obsolete? Does its emotional logic obscure the policy lesson Silber draws? That gap between the film's heart and the economist's ledger is exactly the kind of tension this course wants you to sit with.

Fractional-reserve banking
Banks lend out most deposits, keeping only a fraction as reserves.
Maturity transformation
Borrowing short-term (deposits) to lend long-term (mortgages).
Bank run
A self-reinforcing rush to withdraw that can sink a solvent bank.
Lender of last resort
A central bank that supplies emergency cash to halt a panic.
Deposit insurance
A government guarantee of deposits (the FDIC) that removes the reason to run.

Chapter 8 · The Great Depression

Golden Fetters

Film
The Grapes of Wrath · 1940 · 120 min
Reading
Bernanke · Romer (1993)
The Joads load everything they own onto a broken-down truck and drive west, chasing a handbill's promise of work in California. Behind them, the Oklahoma soil is blowing away; ahead, the promised land is already crowded with the desperate. Tom Joad wants to know who to be angry at, and the film never quite gives him a face — because the force that uprooted his family wasn't a villain. It was a collapse in the money supply.

The Grapes of Wrath shows the Great Depression from the bottom — the human wreckage of the worst economic catastrophe in modern history. Your readings supply what the film cannot: the machinery. Ben Bernanke (a scholar of the Depression long before he chaired the Federal Reserve through the 2008 crisis) and Christina Romer explain how a stock-market crash became a decade-long global collapse, and their answer puts an old villain back on the stand — the gold standard from Chapter 6.

The economic idea

How a recession becomes a depression

Recessions are normal. What turned the 1929 downturn into the Great Depression — a 25% unemployment rate, a 30% fall in output, thousands of bank failures — was a catastrophic contraction of the money supply, roughly a third of it, between 1929 and 1933. As banks failed, deposits vanished; as people hoarded cash, money stopped circulating; and the Federal Reserve, which could have flooded the system with liquidity, largely stood by. Less money chasing goods meant falling prices — deflation — which (as you saw in Chapter 6) crushed every debtor, including farmers like the Joads, and set off more defaults and more bank failures in a vicious spiral.

The reading, unpacked

Bernanke, Romer, and the golden fetters

Why didn't central banks simply print more money? Because most were chained to the gold standard. To hold the gold peg, a country facing gold outflows had to raise interest rates and contract credit — exactly the opposite of what a collapsing economy needed. Economists (Barry Eichengreen coined the phrase "golden fetters," and Bernanke built on it) found a striking pattern: the sooner a country abandoned gold, the sooner it could expand its money supply, halt the deflation, and begin to recover. Britain left in 1931 and recovered early; the United States left in 1933; France clung to gold until 1936 and suffered longest. Romer's work, meanwhile, emphasized how monetary expansion after 1933 — not the New Deal's spending alone — powered the American recovery, and how a premature tightening in 1937 caused a brutal relapse.

✎ The dust and the debt

The film layers two disasters that are easy to confuse. The Dust Bowl was an ecological catastrophe — drought and ruinous farming practices (a resource-economics story from Chapter 1). But what turned ruined harvests into dispossession was financial: mortgaged farms, collapsing crop prices, and banks foreclosing to survive their own crisis. The tractor that levels the Joads' house is sent by a bank that is itself desperate. A strong paper keeps the ecological and monetary threads distinct — and shows how they braided together to put the family on the road.

The film as evidence

The faceless system

The Joads' great frustration — there is no one to shoot, no single villain — is, economically, exactly right. Deflation and monetary contraction are systemic forces with no face. The landowner blames the bank; the bank manager blames the eastern investors; the investors blame the market. Steinbeck and director John Ford dramatize the diffuse, impersonal nature of a monetary collapse better than any textbook. Where the film is weaker, for the economist, is on causes: it renders the Depression as a story of greed and injustice, when Bernanke and Romer show it was substantially a story of policy failure — a preventable contraction of money that better central banking, unshackled from gold, could have softened. The suffering is real and moral; the cause was technical and monetary. Reconciling those two truths is your paper.

Money supply
The total stock of money in an economy; its collapse deepened the Depression.
Deflationary spiral
Falling prices raise real debt, causing defaults, bank failures, and more deflation.
Golden fetters
The gold standard's constraint forcing contraction just when expansion was needed.
Lender of last resort
The role the Fed failed to play adequately from 1930–33.
Debt-deflation
Irving Fisher's mechanism linking falling prices to cascading insolvency.

Chapter 9 · Corporate Social Responsibility

Greed, Good, and the Business of Business

Film
Wall Street · 1987 · 124 min
Reading
Friedman (1970) · Reinhardt, Stavins & Vietor (2008)
"Greed, for lack of a better word, is good. Greed is right. Greed works." Gordon Gekko addresses a shareholders' meeting in a silk suit, and a generation of business students took notes. The line was meant as an indictment. It became, for many, a creed. Underneath the theatrics sits a genuine and unresolved question: what, exactly, is a corporation for?

Oliver Stone made Wall Street to condemn the 1980s culture of the corporate raider, but the film accidentally stages one of the most important debates in economics — the debate between Milton Friedman and the advocates of corporate social responsibility (CSR). Gekko is a grotesque, but he is mouthing a serious position with a serious pedigree, and your two readings are the two sides of the argument he embodies and violates.

The economic idea

Whose interests should a firm serve?

A corporation sits at the center of competing claims: shareholders who own it, workers who staff it, customers who buy from it, communities it affects. Shareholder theory says the firm's managers are agents of the owners and owe their loyalty to maximizing owner value, within the law. Stakeholder theory says the firm owes duties to all who are affected. The CSR debate is a fight over which view is correct — and, more subtly, over whether "doing good" and "doing well" are enemies or allies.

The reading, unpacked

Friedman vs. Reinhardt, Stavins & Vietor

In a famous 1970 New York Times Magazine essay, Milton Friedman argued bluntly that "the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits." His reasoning is not the caricature. A corporate executive, Friedman said, is an agent spending other people's (shareholders') money. When a manager sacrifices profit for a social cause, they are effectively imposing a tax and spending it by their own lights — an unelected official deciding social priorities. Better, he argued, that the firm maximize profit lawfully and ethically, and that citizens and governments handle social goals directly. Crucially, Friedman assumed the firm plays "within the rules of the game" — open, honest competition without fraud.

Reinhardt, Stavins, and Vietor (2008), writing for the Harvard Kennedy School, examine CSR "through an economic lens" and reach a more nuanced verdict. They ask a sharp question: does it ever make economic sense for a firm to sacrifice profits for social ends? Sometimes, yes — when CSR is really disguised profit-seeking (green branding that lifts sales, good labor practices that cut turnover, getting ahead of coming regulation). But genuine, costly CSR that reduces profit is hard to sustain in competitive markets, because it puts the firm at a disadvantage to rivals who don't bother. Their careful conclusion sits between Friedman's absolutism and the boosters' optimism.

ⓘ Gekko isn't Friedman's hero — he's Friedman's nightmare

Students often misread Gekko as the embodiment of Friedman's philosophy. He is the opposite. Friedman's entire argument depends on the firm competing honestly, within the rules of the game, without deception or fraud. Gekko trades on inside information, lies, and loots companies to strip them for parts. He is not a profit-maximizer operating in a fair market; he is a criminal. A precise paper distinguishes lawful shareholder-value maximization (Friedman's actual position) from the fraud the film depicts — and notes that Friedman would want Gekko prosecuted.

The film as evidence

Bluestar Airlines and the raider's calculus

The film's moral center is the fate of Bluestar Airlines, where Bud Fox's father is a union man. Gekko plans to buy the airline and liquidate it — selling the assets for more than the company is worth as a going concern, and gutting the workers' pensions. This is the sharpest CSR case in the film: is "creating shareholder value" by breaking up a functioning company and its community a triumph of efficient markets (assets moving to higher-value uses) or a failure that ignores stakeholders and social cost? Reinhardt and colleagues would ask whether the raid genuinely reallocates resources productively or merely transfers wealth from workers to financiers. Friedman would ask whether it was done lawfully. The film asks whether it was right. Your paper gets to hold all three questions at once.

Chapter 10 · Financial Fraud

The Big Short

Film
The Big Short · 2015 · 130 min
Reading
Bernanke · Mazar & Ariely (2006)
A one-eyed doctor turned hedge-fund manager sits alone, reading thousands of pages of mortgage documents nobody else bothered to open. What he finds is that the foundation of the American economy — the home mortgage — has quietly rotted. He does something that everyone tells him is insane: he bets billions that the housing market will collapse. Then he waits, while the world calls him a fool.

The Big Short is the rare film that tries to teach — pausing to have a celebrity explain a collateralized debt obligation from a bubble bath. It dramatizes the 2007–08 financial crisis, the worst since the Great Depression, and your readings come at it from two levels. Ben Bernanke, then chairing the Federal Reserve, testified on the crisis's structural causes. Nina Mazar and Dan Ariely dig into its human foundation: the ordinary psychology of dishonesty that let millions of small lies add up to a catastrophe.

The economic idea

Bubbles, leverage, and misaligned incentives

An asset bubble forms when prices rise far above fundamental value because everyone expects them to keep rising. Housing in the 2000s was a textbook case, supercharged by three things. Leverage: banks and households borrowed enormously, so small price moves meant huge gains or ruin. Securitization: mortgages were bundled into bonds (and bonds into CDOs), scattering risk so widely that no one felt responsible for any single bad loan. And misaligned incentives at every link — the mortgage broker paid per loan regardless of quality, the bank that sold the loan off its books, the rating agency paid by the very firms whose bonds it graded. Everyone was rewarded for volume, no one for prudence.

Scrub the slider through the years to watch the bubble inflate on cheap optimism — and the contrarian bet, worthless for years, pay off spectacularly when reality arrives.

The reading, unpacked

Bernanke's structure, Ariely's fudge factor

Bernanke's account, in his testimony before the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, is the view from 30,000 feet: a global savings glut and low rates fueled a housing boom; financial innovation spread mortgage risk in ways regulators and firms failed to understand; excessive leverage and short-term funding made institutions fragile; and when housing turned, the interconnected system seized up, requiring extraordinary intervention to prevent a second Depression. It is a story of systemic risk and regulatory blind spots more than of cartoon villains.

Mazar and Ariely supply the missing human layer. In "Dishonesty in Everyday Life," they show experimentally that most people cheat — but only a little, and only when they can still think of themselves as honest. There's a "fudge factor": the amount we'll fib is limited less by the odds of getting caught (Becker's calculus, next chapter) than by our need to maintain a moral self-image. Crucially, dishonesty rises when the lie is one step removed from cash, when "everyone's doing it," and when we can rationalize it. A subprime crisis is built from exactly these conditions: a broker shading an applicant's income, an appraiser nudging a number, a banker not looking too hard — each a small fudge, none feeling like grand theft, all compounding into ruin.

✎ Two theories of cheating

Hold this chapter and the next in tension. Becker (Chapter 11) models dishonesty as cold cost-benefit: people cheat when expected gain beats expected punishment. Mazar and Ariely show that's incomplete — people leave money on the table because their self-image constrains them more than the police do. The 2008 crisis needed both: structures that made small lies profitable and low-risk (Becker), and a culture that let millions rationalize those lies as normal (Ariely). Which mattered more is a genuine open question — and a paper.

The film as evidence

Who understood, and who chose not to

The film's heroes share one trait: they actually read the documents and did the arithmetic that everyone else was incentivized to skip. That is the deepest economic point in the movie — the crisis was less a failure of information than a failure of incentive to use information. When a Florida mortgage broker cheerfully explains he writes loans to strippers with no income because he's paid either way, you are watching Mazar-Ariely's rationalization and Bernanke's misaligned incentives in the same breath. The film's weakness, worth naming, is that its clean hero-villain framing can obscure Bernanke's less satisfying truth: much of the damage came not from evil but from a diffuse system in which prudent behavior was individually unrewarded. Sorting the fraud from the folly is your job.

Bundling loans into tradable securities, dispersing (and hiding) risk.

Chapter 11 · The Economics of Crime

Crime and Punishment, by the Numbers

Film
Blow · 2001 · 124 min
Reading
Becker (1968) · Friedman (1990)
George Jung starts by smuggling a little marijuana to sun-struck friends in California. Within a decade he is moving most of the cocaine that enters the United States, partnered with the Medellín cartel, richer than he can count. The film watches him rise and fall with a kind of awe — and never quite asks the economist's question: if the product were legal, would any of this have happened?

Blow is the true story of a drug trafficker, and it is a perfect case study for the most provocative idea in this course: that crime can be analyzed as a rational choice, and that drug prohibition, in particular, produces the very violence and wealth it aims to prevent. Gary Becker's 1968 paper made crime a subject for economics; Milton Friedman's 1990 open letter turned that analysis into a blistering argument against the war on drugs. Together they will change how you watch every frame.

The economic idea

The criminal as a rational actor

Becker's radical premise: most criminals are not fundamentally different from the rest of us. They respond to costs and benefits. A person commits a crime when its expected benefit exceeds its expected cost — where the expected cost is the probability of being caught and convicted, multiplied by the severity of the punishment. This yields concrete predictions. To deter crime, society can raise either the probability of punishment (more police, better detection) or its severity (longer sentences). And because catching people is expensive while writing harsher laws is cheap, governments are perpetually tempted to substitute severity for certainty — even though evidence suggests certainty deters more reliably.

Move the slider to see the trade-off. As the chance of punishment rises, the number of people for whom crime still "pays" falls — but driving crime to zero would require a police state no society would tolerate or afford.

The reading, unpacked

Friedman: prohibition is the problem

Now apply the model to drugs, and Friedman's argument snaps into focus. Prohibition doesn't eliminate demand; it drives the market underground. There, the risk of arrest and violence becomes a cost of doing business that suppliers must be compensated for — so prohibition acts like an enormous tax that inflates the price and hence the profit. The higher the enforcement, the higher the price, the greater the reward for the traffickers willing to bear the risk. Because contracts can't be enforced in court, disputes are settled with violence, and because the profits are astronomical, they corrupt police, judges, and governments. Friedman's conclusion, in his 1990 open letter to drug czar William Bennett, was that the drug war's harms — violence, mass incarceration, corruption, empowered cartels — are largely created by prohibition itself, and that the humane and effective policy is legalization with regulation and treatment.

The harm done by drugs is predominantly caused not by the drugs themselves but by the fact that they are illegal.
after Milton Friedman, open letter to William Bennett, 1990
ⓘ The prohibition premium

The single most important number in Blow is never stated: the markup. Cocaine costs little to produce; George Jung's fortune is almost entirely the prohibition premium — the price of bearing the risk of arrest and violence. Legalize the product and that premium evaporates, and with it the incentive to build cartels, corrupt officials, and murder rivals. Every helicopter, mansion, and gun in the film is, economically, a monument to the policy of prohibition, not to the pharmacology of the drug.

The film as evidence

A market, not a morality tale

Watch Blow as the story of a supply chain and it transforms. Jung is an entrepreneur exploiting a market with sky-high margins created by the risk of illegality. The violence isn't incidental to the business — it's the enforcement mechanism a black market uses because it can't call the police. Becker would note that Jung keeps operating because, for years, the probability of serious punishment times its severity stayed below his expected haul; his eventual fall comes when that calculus finally flips. Friedman would note that everything terrible in the story — the cartels, the murders, the corruption — is a predictable product of prohibition's economics, not of the substance. The film invites moral judgment of a man; the readings invite economic judgment of a policy. The best papers refuse to collapse one into the other.

Expected punishment
Probability of being caught × severity of the sentence.
Deterrence
Preventing crime by raising its expected cost.
Certainty vs. severity
Evidence suggests the odds of punishment deter more than harshness.
Black market
An illegal market where prohibition inflates prices and violence enforces deals.
Prohibition premium
The extra price (and profit) created purely by a product's illegality.

Chapter 12 · Capital Punishment

Does the Death Penalty Deter?

Film
Dead Man Walking · 1995 · 122 min
Reading
Mocan & Gittings · Levitt (2004)
Sister Helen Prejean becomes the spiritual advisor to a man on death row for a brutal double murder. The film refuses to make it easy: the crime is real and horrifying, the condemned man is neither wholly monster nor wholly innocent, and the victims' families carry a grief no verdict can touch. Into this moral storm, economics asks a cold, narrow, unavoidable question — does killing him prevent other killings?

Dead Man Walking is a film about the morality of capital punishment, and it earns its ambivalence honestly. But this course asks you to add a dimension the film mostly leaves out: the empirical one. Whatever you believe about the ethics, there is a factual question — does the death penalty deter murder? — and it turns out to be one of the hardest questions in all of empirical economics to answer. Your readings are a lesson in how economists argue with data, and in how much humility the arguing requires.

The economic idea

Deterrence meets the limits of data

Becker's logic (Chapter 11) predicts that raising the severity of punishment should reduce crime. The death penalty is the maximum severity, so the model predicts some deterrent effect. But turning that prediction into a measured number is fiendishly hard. You cannot run the experiment. You only observe that states or years with executions also differ in a hundred other ways — poverty, policing, demographics, drug markets. Disentangling the effect of executions from everything else is a problem of causal inference, and reasonable economists, using the same data, have reached opposite conclusions.

The reading, unpacked

Mocan & Gittings vs. the skeptics

Mocan and Gittings ("Getting Off Death Row," 2003) exploit a clever source of variation: not just executions, but commutations and removals from death row. Their reasoning is that if the death penalty deters, then commuting sentences (reducing the expected punishment) should be associated with more murders. Analyzing state data, they report that each execution is associated with several fewer homicides, while commutations and removals are associated with more — a result consistent with a deterrent effect. Other economists (notably in critiques led by John Donohue and Justin Wolfers) argue such findings are fragile: tiny changes in the statistical specification, the sample of states, or the handful of states that do most executing can flip the result, sometimes even to a "brutalization" effect where executions raise murder. The honest scholarly consensus is that the deterrent effect of capital punishment cannot be reliably measured — the data are too noisy and executions too rare to settle it.

Levitt's lesson

Why crime actually fell in the 1990s

Steven Levitt's "Understanding Why Crime Fell in the 1990s" (2004) is your model of how to do this carefully. American crime plunged in the 1990s, and everyone had a pet explanation. Levitt tested them. He credited four factors with real evidence behind them — more police, rising prison populations, the waning of the crack epidemic, and (controversially) legalized abortion — and dismissed six popular explanations that the data don't support, including the strong economy, gun laws, and, notably, capital punishment. His verdict on the death penalty was blunt: executions are so rare relative to murders that even a real deterrent effect would be far too small to explain the crime drop. ✎ Certainty beats severity — again

Notice the through-line from Chapter 11. What deters, in Levitt's data, is certainty — more police and more incarceration raise the probability of punishment for many crimes. The death penalty is the opposite: maximum severity, near-zero probability (a tiny fraction of murderers are ever executed, often decades later). Becker's own model predicts that a punishment applied this rarely can't deter much, because expected punishment is probability times severity, and one of those terms is almost zero.

The film as evidence

What economics can and cannot settle

Dead Man Walking is wise enough to know that the death-penalty debate is not fundamentally about deterrence — it is about justice, retribution, mercy, and the meaning of a life. Economics cannot resolve those questions and shouldn't pretend to. What it can do is remove one common argument from the table: if the empirical case for deterrence is as weak as Levitt and the skeptics suggest, then "it saves innocent lives by deterring murder" is a much shakier justification than its proponents believe. That narrows the debate to the moral core the film so carefully explores. A strong paper draws exactly that line — using the readings to say what economics settles, and where it must fall silent and let ethics speak.

Chapter 13 · Labor Unions

The Price of an Hour

Film
Norma Rae · 1979 · 113 min
Reading
Whaples (1990)
A textile worker climbs onto her table on the mill floor and holds up a hand-lettered sign that says, simply, UNION. One by one, the machines go silent as her coworkers stop to read it. It is one of the great images in American film — and it is a picture of workers solving a coordination problem that no single one of them could solve alone.

Norma Rae tells the story of a Southern textile worker who risks everything to organize her mill. It is a labor drama, but underneath it lies the economics of the labor market: why wages are what they are, whether a single dominant employer can suppress them, and how collective bargaining changes the arithmetic of work. Robert Whaples's study of "Winning the Eight-Hour Day" grounds the film in the long, concrete history of how workers actually won the conditions we now take for granted.

The economic idea

Monopsony: when there's only one buyer of labor

In a competitive labor market, many employers bid for workers, and the wage is pushed up to the value a worker adds. But a mill town often has effectively one major employer — a condition economists call monopsony (a single buyer, the mirror image of monopoly). A monopsonist can pay below the competitive wage, because workers have nowhere else to go. This is the classic economic case where a union — or a minimum wage — can raise pay without destroying jobs, because it simply claws back the wage the employer was suppressing. It is one of the few places in economics where "intervene in the market" and "efficiency" can point the same direction.

Move the slider through possible wages and watch the gap between how many want to work and how many the mill will hire — and see where a union could push without costing jobs.

The reading, unpacked

Whaples: how the eight-hour day was won

Robert Whaples's "Winning the Eight-Hour Day, 1909–1919" examines one of labor's signature achievements — the reduction of the standard workday — and asks why it happened when it did. His analysis untangles the forces: rising productivity and incomes (as workers grew richer, they "bought" more leisure), tight wartime labor markets that raised workers' bargaining power, union pressure and strikes, and government action. The lesson for the film is that improvements in working conditions were not simply handed down by enlightened employers, nor won by heroism alone. They emerged from bargaining power — shifting with the economy, the labor supply, and the ability of workers to act collectively. Norma Rae's mill is a snapshot of that struggle at a moment and place where workers' power was still being fought for.

ⓘ Why one worker can't bargain

The economic heart of the film is a collective-action problem. Any single worker who demands better pay is simply fired and replaced — the employer holds all the power in a one-on-one negotiation. Workers gain leverage only by acting together, credibly threatening to withhold their labor as a group. But organizing is dangerous and costly, and each worker is tempted to let others take the risk (free-riding, Chapter 1 again). Norma Rae's sign on the table is the moment the coordination problem is solved — a single visible act that lets everyone see that everyone else is ready to act too. It is Chapter 7's bank-run logic run in reverse: shifting the equilibrium from "everyone stays silent" to "everyone stands up."

The film as evidence

Power, not just principle

Watch Norma Rae for its economics and you'll notice it gets the power dynamics exactly right. The mill uses every tool of a monopsonist and a determined employer: surveillance, the threat of firing, stoking division among workers (including along racial lines), and speed-ups. The union's strategy is precisely to change the workers' bargaining power by solving their coordination problem. Whaples reminds us that the outcome of such struggles has always depended on the surrounding economics — how tight the labor market is, how productive the industry, whether the law protects organizing. The film's triumphant ending is real, but Whaples's history warns against romanticizing it: the eight-hour day and the union contract were won and sometimes lost as economic conditions shifted. A fine paper honors the courage on screen while insisting on the economic forces that made that courage effective — or that could just as easily have defeated it.

The improvements were won, not granted — and won when the economics of bargaining power turned in labor's favor.
the lesson of Whaples's history, applied to Norma Rae's mill
